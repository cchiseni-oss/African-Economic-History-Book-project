## 1. Summary

Africa's economic history has grown quickly over the past fifteen years. New archives, digitised colonial records and statistical methods have reopened old questions about the slave trades, colonial rule, state capacity and growth after independence, and the African Economic History Network's annual meeting went from a handful of participants in 2010 to more than 100 by 2019. Most of this research is written by scholars based outside Africa: of the papers on Africa published in the top four economic history journals in the two decades to 2019, 13.5 per cent were written by scholars based in Africa (Fourie 2019).

*From Within* brings together African economic historians, on the continent and in the diaspora, to write the continent's economic history from African vantage points. Sixteen thematic chapters cover the main development themes of the field: pre-colonial production, trade and money; the slave trades; colonial taxation, labour, export agriculture and human capital; planning after independence; debt and structural adjustment; gender; monetary sovereignty; infrastructure; climate shocks; and continental integration. An introduction makes the case for the volume and sets out its method. A conclusion proposes an agenda for the field.

What the contributors share is a method. They start from questions that matter in African societies. They combine quantitative evidence with African archives, oral histories and African-language sources. They treat Africans as economic actors, attend to the continent's regional and linguistic diversity, including Francophone, Lusophone and North Africa, and engage with African intellectual traditions from the Ibadan and Dar es Salaam schools to CODESRIA and recent data-driven work. Where contributors disagree, the volume shows the disagreement.

Each chapter can be read on its own and used in teaching. Chapters include a short "Sources and silences" box, discussion questions and annotated further reading, so that the book can serve as a core text in African universities as well as a reference for researchers and policymakers. The editors intend to publish the book open access, with an affordable print edition for African readers.

## 2. Why this book is needed

### 2.1 Who writes Africa's economic history

Studies of authorship in economic history and neighbouring fields show the same pattern.

- **Economic history.** Of the papers on Africa in the top four economic history journals over the two decades to 2019, 13.5 per cent were written by scholars based in Africa (Fourie 2019).
- **African studies.** The share of articles by Africa-based authors in *African Affairs* and the *Journal of Modern African Studies* fell from about 30 per cent in 1993 to about 15 per cent in 2013. The authors traced the fall to low and declining acceptance rates rather than to fewer submissions, and found that Africa-based scholars were cited less than others (Briggs and Weathers 2016).
- **Economics.** A quarter of the economics journal articles on Africa in Chelwa's sample had at least one Africa-based author, while Africa-based researchers held about 3 per cent of the editorial-board seats of those journals (Chelwa 2021).
- **Development studies.** Researchers based in the Global South wrote just over a quarter of the publications in top development journals (Amarante et al. 2022).
- **Country coverage.** Five countries accounted for 65 per cent of the articles on Africa in the top five economics journals between 2000 and 2019, and for 45 per cent of the articles on Africa across all economics journals (Porteous 2022).

These studies measure where authors work, not where they come from. Many African economic historians are based in Europe and North America. This volume includes them and gives priority to scholars based on the continent.

### 2.2 Why authorship matters

Who writes history shapes which questions are asked, which sources are trusted and which conclusions reach policy. Critics of the recent literature have pointed to three recurring problems (see, for example, Austin 2008; Hopkins 2009).

1. **Questions set elsewhere.** Much recent work asks why Africa is poorer than other regions and uses African cases to test general theories. Questions about how African households, firms and states managed risk, invested and governed receive less attention.
2. **Compressed history.** Studies that link a distant cause, such as the slave trades or colonial institutions, directly to present-day outcomes can skip the centuries in between, during which African societies changed (Austin 2008).
3. **Thin sources.** Colonial records and European observers remain the default evidence. Oral histories, African-language sources and local archives are under-used, although working with them often requires the language skills and local networks that many African scholars have.

### 2.3 Traditions to build on

African scholars have written economic history since the field took shape on the continent. At Ibadan, Kenneth Onwuka Dike's *Trade and Politics in the Niger Delta, 1830–1885* (1956) put African traders and states at the centre of West African history. At the University of Dar es Salaam, scholars developed a political economy of underdevelopment: Walter Rodney, the Guyanese historian who taught there, wrote *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972), and Abdul Sheriff wrote *Slaves, Spices and Ivory in Zanzibar* (1987). CODESRIA, founded in Dakar in 1973, published Paul Tiyambe Zeleza's *A Modern Economic History of Africa, Volume 1: The Nineteenth Century* (1993), which won the 1994 Noma Award for Publishing in Africa. The UNESCO *General History of Africa*, eight volumes published by 1993 and three more released in 2025, showed that a continental history written largely by African scholars could be done.

More recently, the quantitative turn has brought new data and methods into the field, and the African Economic History Network's open-access textbook, *The History of African Development*, has widened access for students. *From Within* joins these strands. It pairs the questions and sources of the earlier African schools with the tools of the new economic history, and puts African authorship at its centre.

### 2.4 Why now

- **The field is growing and meeting in Africa.** The AEHN annual meeting, which passed 100 participants by 2019 (Fourie 2019), has been held on the continent, for example at the University of Pretoria in 2023 (AEHN 2023).
- **Policy debates rest on historical claims.** Arguments about debt (Zambia defaulted in 2020 and restructured under the G20 Common Framework), state capacity, industrial policy, the African Continental Free Trade Area and climate risk all draw on readings of the past. Those readings should include African evidence and African authors.
- **The means exist.** Digitised archives, open-access publishing and a new cohort of African economic historians make a volume of this kind feasible now.

## 3. Aims

1. Give a broad, African-authored account of the main development themes in Africa's economic history, from pre-colonial times to the present.
2. Show what an Africa-centred method looks like in practice: in the questions asked, the sources used and the concepts applied.
3. Provide students and teachers in African universities with an open-access core text and teaching material.
4. Build a network of African economic historians across regions and languages, with early-career scholars mentored by senior colleagues.

## 4. What "African perspectives" means in this volume

The volume treats "African perspectives" as a set of commitments that each chapter can be checked against.

**Editorial principles**

- **Questions from African experience.** Chapters start from problems that mattered to the people involved: how communities shared risk, how rulers raised revenue, how households paid for schooling. Comparison with other world regions is one lens among several.
- **African evidence.** Chapters combine quantitative data with national, provincial, chiefdom, mission and court records, oral histories, African-language sources (for example Arabic and Ajami manuscripts, and texts in Swahili and Amharic) and material evidence. They explain the limits of colonial-era statistics.
- **African agency.** Africans appear as farmers, traders, miners, migrants, savers, rulers, workers and resisters, with attention to differences of gender, class, region and generation.
- **History in full.** Chapters that link the past to present outcomes trace the mechanisms through the intervening periods.
- **Plural methods.** Cliometric, qualitative and mixed-methods chapters sit side by side.
- **Engagement with African scholarship.** Each chapter draws on African scholarship, including work in French, Portuguese and Arabic and work published by African presses.
- **Continental scope.** The volume covers North, West, Central, East and Southern Africa, and Anglophone, Francophone, Lusophone and Arabic-speaking regions. Chapters that focus on one region say so.

**Who contributes**

- Lead authors are African scholars, by nationality or heritage, based on the continent or in the diaspora. Co-authors from elsewhere are welcome where they bring specific expertise.
- Targets: at least half of lead authors based at African institutions; at least 40 per cent women; all five regions represented; at least three chapters led by scholars from Francophone, Lusophone or Arabic-speaking countries; at least a quarter of contributors at an early career stage, each paired with a senior mentor.
- The introduction will report the volume's actual authorship against these targets.

**What every chapter includes**

- 6,500–7,500 words, including notes and references.
- A 300-word "Sources and silences" box on the evidence used and what it leaves out.
- Three to five discussion questions and five annotated further readings, at least two of them by African scholars.
- Two to four maps, figures or tables.
- Abstracts in English and French, and in Portuguese or Arabic where relevant.
- Data and code deposited in an open repository where permissions allow.

**Chapter 1. Introduction: Writing Africa's Economic Past from Within**

*Michael Chanda Chiseni [and co-editor]*

Sets out the authorship gap and its consequences, traces the African traditions on which the book builds, defines the editorial principles and previews the chapters. Maps the debates that run between chapters: the weight of external and internal forces, colonial legacies and post-colonial choices, and the uses and limits of quantitative studies of long-run persistence.

### Part I. Knowledge, Sources and Method

**Chapter 2. From Ibadan and Dar es Salaam to the Data Revolution: African Traditions in Economic History**

How African scholars have written economic history since the 1950s: nationalist historiography at Ibadan, political economy at Dar es Salaam, dependency and Marxian analysis, CODESRIA's institution-building, the squeeze on African universities during structural adjustment, and the recent quantitative revival. Asks why African scholars became less visible just as the field grew, and what the new economic history has gained and lost as a result.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* African historiography or intellectual history.

**Chapter 3. Sources, Silences and Numbers: Evidence for an Africa-Centred Economic History**

Surveys the evidence base: national and provincial archives; chiefdom, mission and court records; oral histories; Arabic and Ajami manuscripts; newspapers; archaeological evidence; and colonial and post-colonial statistics. Discusses whose categories shape historical datasets, how to build data from African sources, and the practical problems of preserving, accessing and digitising archives on the continent.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* archival or quantitative history, with experience of building datasets from African archives.

### Part II. African Economies Before Colonial Rule

**Chapter 4. Land, Labour and Ecological Knowledge: Production Before Colonial Rule**

How farmers, herders and craft producers organised production in very different environments: shifting cultivation, intercropping and irrigation, iron-working and textiles, and the adoption of American crops such as maize and cassava. Re-examines, with African evidence, the argument that abundant land and scarce labour shaped African institutions, and asks how ecological knowledge affected resilience and inequality.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* agrarian or environmental history.

**Chapter 5. Markets, Merchants, Money and States: Africa's Commercial Networks**

Trans-Saharan, Nile, Sahelian, Swahili-coast and Atlantic trade; merchant communities such as Hausa, Juula and Swahili traders; and the institutions that made long-distance trade work, including credit, brokerage, contract enforcement and currencies such as cowries, cloth, gold dust and copper. Places North Africa and the Sahel in one continental story.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* history of trade, ideally with Arabic or Ajami sources.

**Chapter 6. The Slave Trades from Within: Demographic, Political and Economic Consequences**

The Atlantic, trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean slave trades as experienced by African societies: how states, merchants and communities took part, resisted and adapted; the demographic and gendered consequences; and the nineteenth-century shift to commodity exports. Engages with quantitative studies of long-run effects by asking which mechanisms African sources support.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* history of slavery or historical demography.

### Part III. Colonial Economies and African Responses

**Chapter 7. Taxing Subjects: Colonial States, Chiefs and Fiscal Extraction**

Hut and poll taxes, compulsory labour, chiefs as tax collectors under indirect rule, and how colonial revenue was spent, compared across British, French, Portuguese and Belgian territories. Examines African responses: evasion, migration, petitions and revolts such as the 1929 Women's War in south-eastern Nigeria.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* fiscal history or political economy.

**Chapter 8. Mines, Migrants and Compounds: Labour Regimes in Colonial Africa**

Recruitment, wages, compounds and health on the Witwatersrand and the Copperbelt, in West African gold mining and on plantations; and how sending regions such as Nyasaland and Basutoland reorganised household economies around migration.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* labour history of southern or central Africa.

**Chapter 9. Peasants, Planters and Marketing Boards: Export Agriculture and African Agency**

African-led export booms such as Gold Coast cocoa and Senegambian groundnuts; forced cotton cultivation in Mozambique and the Belgian Congo; settler agriculture in Kenya and Southern Rhodesia; and the marketing boards that taxed farmers well into independence. Asks who captured the gains from export growth and how producers responded.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* agrarian history of West or East Africa.

**Chapter 10. Missions, Schools and Clinics: Human Capital and Its Unequal Legacies**

*Michael Chanda Chiseni*

Drawing on the editor's research on Zambia and on comparative evidence, examines how missions and colonial states provided schooling and health care, why provision was uneven by region and gender, and how African demand, teachers and catechists shaped outcomes. Follows these patterns through the expansion of schooling after independence to present-day inequality.

### Part IV. Independence, Crisis and Reform

**Chapter 11. Planning Independence: Nationalisation, African Socialism and the Developmental State**

Compares strategies after independence: industrialisation in Nkrumah's Ghana, Ujamaa in Tanzania, Zambian Humanism and the nationalisation of the copper mines, Nigeria's indigenisation decrees, Côte d'Ivoire's export-agriculture model and Botswana's mineral-led growth. Treats the ideas of African economists and planners, such as Adebayo Adedeji and Samir Amin, as historical evidence.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* history of development policy.

**Chapter 12. Debt, Adjustment and Policy Sovereignty: From the Lagos Plan to the Common Framework**

African debates over policy autonomy, from the Organisation of African Unity's Lagos Plan of Action (1980) and the World Bank's Berg Report (1981), through structural adjustment and the UN Economic Commission for Africa's alternative framework (1989), to debt relief under the HIPC initiative and the debt crises of the 2020s, including Zambia's default and restructuring. Asks what adjustment changed and how governments and citizens contested it.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* economic history or political economy of debt and policy.

**Chapter 13. Women's Work, Markets and Property: Gender in Africa's Economic History**

Women as farmers, traders, workers and household managers; market women's associations; marriage payments and household economies; land and inheritance rights; and colonial and post-colonial policies that changed women's access to schooling, credit and property. Uses oral histories and court records to recover women's economic lives.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* gender history.

### Part V. Long-Run Trajectories: Africa and the World

**Chapter 14. Money and Monetary Sovereignty: From Currency Boards and the CFA Franc to Mobile Money**

Colonial currency boards (the West African Currency Board from 1912 and the East African Currency Board from 1919), the founding of central banks after independence, the CFA franc zone and the reform debate since 2019, savings groups such as *susu*, *chilimba* and stokvels, and the spread of mobile money since 2007. Asks how monetary arrangements narrowed or widened development choices.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* monetary or financial history, ideally including Francophone Africa.

**Chapter 15. Roads, Rails and Regions: Infrastructure and Spatial Inequality**

Colonial railways built to carry minerals and crops to the coast; post-independence projects such as the Kariba and Akosombo dams and the TAZARA railway; and recent Chinese-financed infrastructure. Uses spatial data and local evidence to ask who benefited and how infrastructure shaped regional inequality.

*Contributor:* [TBC; the editor may co-author, drawing on his research on roads and household wealth in Africa]. *Expertise sought:* economic history or geography of infrastructure.

**Chapter 16. Climate, Famine and Adaptation: Environmental Shocks in the Long Run**

Droughts, locust invasions, the rinderpest epidemic of the 1890s, the Sahelian droughts of the 1970s and 1980s, and the Ethiopian famine of 1983–85: how communities, states and markets responded, and what these histories suggest for climate adaptation today.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* environmental or climate history.

**Chapter 17. From Customs Unions to the AfCFTA: Pan-African Integration in the World Economy**

Regional integration from the Southern African Customs Union (1910) and the first East African Community (1967–77) to ECOWAS (1975), SADC (1992) and the African Continental Free Trade Area (trading since 2021), set against commodity dependence and Africa's changing external partners. Asks why integration has been difficult and what earlier attempts teach.

*Contributor:* [TBC]. *Expertise sought:* trade history or international economics.

**Chapter 18. Conclusion: An Agenda for African Economic History**

*The editors*

Draws the findings together, identifies under-studied regions and themes, and sets out priorities for archives, data, training and publishing on the continent.

**Reserve topics.** Depending on the response to the call, the editors may add or substitute chapters on urbanisation and the informal economy; African business history and entrepreneurship; Islamic economic institutions such as waqf endowments and Sufi trading networks; land tenure and land reform; demography and health; war and economic change; migration and remittances; and North Africa and the Nile valley, if other chapters do not cover them adequately.

## 6. Readership and market

- **Courses.** Undergraduate and postgraduate courses in African history, economic history, development economics and development studies, in Africa and elsewhere. The teaching material in each chapter is designed for course adoption.
- **Researchers.** Economic historians, economists, historians, geographers and political scientists working on Africa.
- **Policy readers.** Staff of the African Union Commission, the African Development Bank, the UN Economic Commission for Africa, central banks, ministries of finance and planning, and development agencies.
- **General readers** interested in Africa's economic past and its present choices.

Open access and an affordable print edition matter for this readership, because imported academic books are expensive for most African students and libraries. A French edition would reach Francophone universities; the AEHN textbook's French edition is a precedent.
